The shift

What changes, and what doesn't

People still decide what to build and what is acceptable. What changes is who types the code, and how our standards get enforced.

Key concept

What is a "harness"?

The AI model is the engine. The harness is everything around it that steers it, limits it and checks its work, like a capable new hire working inside our processes.

The harness, set up by us
InstructionsOur conventions, architecture and "how we do things here"
ContextThe codebase, docs, approved patterns and examples
Tools & permissionsWhat the agent may read, run or change, and what it may not
Rules & lintersAutomatic checks for code quality, privacy and security
AI agentwrites the code
TestsProof the app still does what the business needs
Review gatesA person approves before anything ships
PipelineThe same checks run again before merge and deploy
Audit trailA record of what changed, why, and who approved it
Most of the harness is made of things we already know: coding standards, code review, automated tests, CI/CD. What's new is that the AI agent reads and follows them on every change.
Common question

Do we rebuild the harness for every app? No.

It works like building codes. The city writes the code once, every house follows it, and each house adds only what is specific to it. We build the harness in layers, and each app inherits from the layers above.

Organization baseline
Standard linters and rulesets, privacy and security rules, approved tools and permissions, pipeline checks
Built once, then maintained
Team / platform
Shared config for a language or stack: rule severities, approved data layer, common patterns
Per stack, occasionally tuned
Individual app
App-specific instructions and context, its own tests, any exceptions (documented and approved)
Small, per app
Every new app starts with most of the guardrails already in place. A rule added to the baseline protects every app at once. The harness gets better over time; it doesn't start from zero each time.
Rules in the harness

Mostly we pick and configure. We write only what's specific to us.

"Linting rules" are automatic checks that read code and flag problems before a person ever sees it. There are three levels of effort.

Use as-is

Standard linters

The standard linter for each language, with a community or vendor ruleset.

Covers the generic, mechanical checks. No need to reinvent them.

Configure

Tune to our standards

Turn rules on or off and set the severity: warning or blocks the merge.

A team lead or platform engineer does this once in a shared config, and apps inherit it.

Write custom

Rules only we need

Usually just a handful, for things unique to our business and our data.

Agents can draft them; a person who understands the risk decides what they are.

Custom rules

Turning our "never do this" list into automatic enforcement

Today our most important rules live in policy documents and people's heads. In a harness they become checks that run on every change, whether a person or an agent wrote the code.

Example

"Never log a claimant identifier"

Protects personal data from leaking into log files that many people and systems can read.

Example

"All database access goes through the approved data layer"

Keeps access control, auditing and data handling consistent across every app.

Few in number, high in value. Writing them is cheap. Choosing the right ones takes judgment about the business and its risks.

How it works day to day

How a change moves through the harness

Every change passes the same gates, whether a person or an agent wrote it. Problems are caught in seconds, not in production.

1 · PEOPLEDefine the workBusiness need and acceptance criteria
→
2 · AGENTDraft the changeFollowing our instructions and context
→
3 · AUTOMATICRules & tests runLinters, custom rules, tests. The agent fixes what fails.
→
4 · PEOPLEHuman reviewIs this the right change? Is the risk acceptable?
→
5 · PIPELINEMerge & deployChecks run again; full audit trail kept
A rule set to "blocks the merge" can't be skipped by an agent or a person in a hurry. That is how the privacy and security rules become guaranteed rather than hoped-for.
Where non-coders contribute

The most important decisions happen before any code is written

Business and governance leaders contribute upstream of the code: deciding which rules matter. Engineers and agents turn those decisions into enforcement.

Highest value

Privacy & security rules

What must never be:

  • logged
  • stored
  • exposed

Get these from our privacy and security people, then have engineers encode them.

Also valuable

Business rules & boundaries

  • Which data is sensitive, and why
  • Which systems are the approved source of truth
  • Which mistakes would be most costly to customers or regulators
Recommended next steps

Getting started

Small, concrete steps. Each one builds the shared harness that every future app inherits.

  1. Name an owner for the shared harness baseline.Platform engineering / IT
  2. Adopt the standard linters and rulesets for our main languages, in one shared config.Platform engineer or team lead
  3. Hold a short working session to produce our "never log, store or expose" list.Privacy & security, with business owners
  4. Encode the top handful of those as custom rules that block the merge.Engineers (agents draft, humans approve)
  5. Pilot it on one app, measure what gets caught, then roll the baseline out to others.One delivery team, reporting back to this group
